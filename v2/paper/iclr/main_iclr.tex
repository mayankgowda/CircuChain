\title{The Clockwise Prior: Silent, Directed Convention Reversion in Language Models}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Many quantities in science and engineering carry a sign that is fixed only by
convention: a mesh current is positive if it circulates clockwise, a line integral is
positive if the curve is traversed counter-clockwise, a cross product follows the
right hand. Both choices are always physically valid, so an instruction to use the
non-standard choice is legitimate, unambiguous, and easy to state. We study what
language models do with such instructions. Across three physics and mathematics
domains whose answers are verified to the sign by four independent solvers, eight
instructable conventions, and fifteen model configurations, we find that when models
get these signs wrong, they do not fail randomly. Among \totalDiagVars{} graded
variables, \coherencePct\% of all sign errors reproduce, exactly, the value that the
\emph{trained} convention would give; on the seven conventions whose transform is a
pure sign flip, the figure is 100\%. We call this failure mode \emph{reversion}, and
we map what governs it. First, reversion tracks how the convention's reversal is
taught: the one convention whose reversal is absent from textbooks (counter-clockwise
mesh currents) defeats every frontier model we test (\ccwRate\% reversion), while
conventions whose reversal is itself part of the curriculum are obeyed
($\le$\rxnRate\%). Second, task difficulty reactivates the prior: within a domain
that models obey when problems are easy, hardening the problems raises reversion from
\cwEGptOss\% to \cwHGemma\%, while a control instruction demanding the identical
output change stays below 1\%. Third, compliance is capability-gated: mid-scale
models revert on conventions frontier models obey. Mitigations help less than one
would hope. Enabling a reasoning mode raises accuracy dramatically while leaving
reversion nearly unchanged on most conventions, restoring compliance only where the
reversal is both taught and procedurally shallow; and placing fully worked examples
of the required convention in the prompt leaves reversion essentially unchanged in
two of three frontier models. Two predictions we pre-registered were refuted by the
data, and we report both. The benchmark regenerates deterministically from seeds and
uses no language-model judges anywhere.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Ask a language model to analyze a simple circuit, but add one sentence: ``mesh
currents are defined counter-clockwise.'' Circuit textbooks almost universally draw
mesh currents clockwise, but nothing in physics prefers one direction; the choice
only fixes which currents are reported as positive. A model that follows the
instruction produces the same magnitudes as usual with certain signs flipped. What we
observe, across every capable model we test, is different: the model sets up correct
equations, solves them correctly, recovers every magnitude, and then reports the
signs that a \emph{clockwise} analysis would have produced. The response contains no
warning and no inconsistency a reader could notice. Unless the answer is checked
against a solver that knows which convention was requested, the substitution is
invisible.

This paper names that behavior, measures it at scale, identifies three factors that
govern it, and tests two mitigations that mostly fail to fix it. Throughout, we use
one term for the central phenomenon, defined precisely in
Section~\ref{sec:defs}: a model \emph{reverts} on a variable when its answer has the
correct magnitude but carries the sign of the training-default convention rather
than the instructed one. Reversion is a compliance failure that standard evaluations
cannot see, because they either grade magnitudes, accept answers up to sign, or use
language-model judges that share the same training priors.

Sign conventions turn out to be an unusually clean instrument for separating
\emph{competence} (can the model solve the problem?) from \emph{compliance} (does the
instruction win when it conflicts with training?). Three properties make them so.
They are \emph{arbitrary}: both choices are physically valid, so the instruction is
never a trick. They are \emph{binary}: on any single variable, compliance is a sign.
And they are \emph{exactly gradable}: a conventional solver can compute the correct
answer under either convention, so no judgment call, rubric, or judge model is
involved. Because magnitude and sign are graded separately, a single response yields
independent measurements of both axes.

\textbf{What we find.} Our results are organized as four claims.
(1)~Reversion is \emph{silent and directed} (Section~\ref{sec:directed}): sign errors
almost never land on arbitrary values; they land on the trained convention's value,
variable by variable, in \coherencePct\% of \totalDiagVars{} graded cases.
(2)~Reversion is governed by three measurable factors
(Section~\ref{sec:anatomy}): how the convention's \emph{reversal} is represented in
pedagogy, how hard the problem is, and how capable the model is.
(3)~Mitigation is conditional at best (Section~\ref{sec:nofix}): reasoning modes buy
large competence gains and, on most conventions, no compliance; worked in-context
examples buy almost nothing anywhere.
(4)~The framework is falsifiable, and was falsified twice
(Section~\ref{sec:prereg}): two predictions recorded in the project's version history
before data collection came out opposite to what we predicted, and both refutations
are reported and interpreted.

\textbf{Why it matters.} A sign is the difference between tension and compression,
charging and discharging, stable and unstable. The failure mode we document is
worst-case shaped: it is invisible in the answer text, it points consistently toward
the training distribution, it grows when problems get harder, and it resists the
first mitigation a practitioner would try. Evaluations that do not grade signs
against the instructed frame will report such a model as excellent.

\section{Definitions and instrument}\label{sec:defs}

\subsection{Conventions, contracts, and cells}\label{sec:contracts}
A \emph{convention} is a sign or orientation choice that is fixed by custom rather
than physics. A \emph{convention contract} is the block of an instance's prompt that
states which conventions the answer must use. Every instance in our benchmark states
its contract explicitly, including the default one; models are never expected to
guess. A \emph{cell} is a version of the same underlying physics problem posed under
a particular contract. The \emph{default cell} states the textbook conventions. A
\emph{flip cell} changes exactly one convention and leaves everything else
identical. Because the physics is shared, a model's behavior on the default cell and
a flip cell of the same problem forms a matched pair, and causal claims about the
flipped convention come from within-problem comparisons rather than across-problem
averages.

A concrete example makes the design tangible. One circuits instance shows a
five-element network and states, among other conventions, ``mesh currents
($i_1, i_2, i_3$) are defined CLOCKWISE'' (the default cell) or ``\ldots defined
COUNTER-CLOCKWISE, i.e., the reverse of the usual clockwise choice'' (the flip
cell). The requested output is a fixed line of \texttt{name=value} pairs. Under the
flip, the correct values of $i_1, i_2, i_3$ are the negations of their default-cell
values; every other requested quantity is unchanged. A model that answers the flip
cell with the default cell's signs has followed its training rather than its
instructions, and the grader can detect this exactly.

\subsection{Reversion, precisely}\label{sec:reversion}
For every instance we compute two reference answers with conventional solvers: the
\emph{instructed answer} (correct under the stated contract) and the \emph{default
answer} (correct under the textbook contract). A variable is \emph{diagnostic} for a
cell when these two answers differ in sign; only diagnostic variables can reveal
convention behavior, and all rates in this paper are computed over them. A model's
value for a diagnostic variable is graded into exactly one of four categories:
\emph{compliant} (matches the instructed answer within a 5\% relative tolerance),
\emph{reverted} (magnitude correct but the value matches the \emph{default} answer),
\emph{incoherent} (magnitude correct but the sign matches neither reference), or
\emph{magnitude-wrong} (a competence error, excluded from compliance rates). The
\emph{reversion rate} of a model on a cell is the fraction of its magnitude-correct
diagnostic variables that are reverted. Conditioning on magnitude-correctness is
what separates the axes: a model can only revert on a variable it was competent
enough to compute.

\subsection{Three domains, eight conventions}\label{sec:domains}
\emph{Circuits}: linear DC networks across seven topology families; requested
variables are mesh currents, node voltages, and branch quantities. Its three
conventions are mesh direction (default clockwise), the passive sign convention, and
the reference node. \emph{Contour}: line integrals of polynomial vector fields
around lattice polygons; requested variables are per-edge work integrals, the total
circulation, and the flux. Its conventions are traversal orientation (default
counter-clockwise), the sign of reported work, and the flux normal. \emph{Vectors}:
multi-body torque, angular-momentum, and magnetic-force problems in three
dimensions. Its conventions are cross-product handedness (default right-hand rule)
and the Newton's-third-law reporting choice. Difficulty in every domain is a
generator setting (element count, polynomial degree, coordinate size), and each
domain ships an easy and a hardened tier.

Two design choices matter for later sections. First, the contour cells for
clockwise orientation (\texttt{cw}) and for reporting work done \emph{against} the
field (\texttt{wrk}) demand the \emph{byte-identical} output transform: negate every
work value. They differ only in which convention is named. Any behavioral difference
between them therefore isolates the identity of the convention with the output
demand perfectly matched; we use this as a control against ``models just get sloppy
under load.'' Second, each domain also poses every problem under two required
solution procedures (mesh vs.\ nodal analysis; direct integration vs.\ Green's
theorem; direct summation vs.\ the transfer theorem), which lets us check that
reversion is a property of the \emph{reported frame}, not of any particular solving
procedure.

\subsection{Verification and grading}\label{sec:verification}
Every accepted instance is solved by four independent routes before any model sees
it: in circuits, two independently hand-derived formulations, an exact symbolic
modified-nodal-analysis solver, and the NGSPICE simulator; in contour, symbolic
integration of each parametrized edge, Green's-theorem double integration over an
exact triangulation, an independently implemented rational-arithmetic engine, and
numerical quadrature; in vectors, textbook component formulas, Levi-Civita
summation, symbolic cross products, and floating-point evaluation, together with
exact transfer-theorem identities. Three of the four routes use exact rational
arithmetic and must agree to the bit; instances are rejected otherwise, so the
released datasets contain zero solver disagreements. The instructed answers for
every flip cell are additionally validated by \emph{independent physical
realization} rather than by re-applying the same transform: the counter-clockwise
circuit cell by re-grounded re-simulation, the left-handed cell by re-solving the
entire problem with the Levi-Civita symbol negated, the reaction cell by negating
the force sources (125/125 problems pass in each domain). Grading is a deterministic
parser over a mandated answer line. No language model participates in generation,
verification, grading, or analysis.

\subsection{Model panel}\label{sec:panel}
We evaluate fifteen configurations: GPT-5 at low reasoning effort, gpt-oss-120b,
Gemma-4-31B, DeepSeek-V3, Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen2.5-72B, and the Qwen3 dense family
(1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B, 32B), each Qwen3 size in both its reasoning and non-reasoning
mode, which toggles behavior while holding weights fixed. Whole-problem competence
spans roughly 790$\times$ across the panel. Decoding settings are uniform;
serving stack and quantization are pinned and recorded per response. We refer to
GPT-5, gpt-oss-120b, and Gemma-4-31B as the \emph{frontier} models: they are the
only panel members that solve the majority of problems in every domain, and
several claims are therefore stated for them separately.

\section{Reversion is silent and directed}\label{sec:directed}
Our first result is about the \emph{structure} of sign errors, and it holds
everywhere we look. Across all domains, tiers, cells, and models, we grade
\totalDiagVars{} magnitude-correct diagnostic variables. If sign errors were noise,
errors would scatter between the default frame and incoherent values. They do not:
\coherencePct\% of all sign errors equal the trained convention's value exactly. The
\incoherentN{} incoherent cases (0.15\%) all occur in a single place, the circuits
reference-node cell, which is the one convention in the study whose transform is
affine (subtract a reference potential) rather than a sign flip; a model that
applies the shift partially lands between frames. On all seven pure sign-flip
conventions, spanning tens of thousands of variables, the incoherence count is
exactly zero. When these models get a sign wrong, they are not guessing. They are
reproducing their training distribution, one variable at a time.

The matched-pair design makes the effect causal rather than correlational. In
circuits, posing the same problem under the counter-clockwise contract instead of
the default breaks 401 previously-solved problems across the panel while fixing 6;
exact McNemar tests on these within-problem pairs are significant at
$q_{\mathrm{BH}}\le 0.006$ in every competent family. Competence does not protect a
model: Gemma-4-31B solves \circMagGemma\% of circuit magnitudes and still reverts on
\circCcwGemma\% of its diagnostic signs. Nor is this a general inability to follow
unusual instructions: the same models comply at 85--97\% with matched instructions
that are equally unusual but touch no convention (e.g., reporting variables in
reverse order), and re-sampling the entire measurement five times moves the headline
rates by only a few points.

\section{What governs reversion}\label{sec:anatomy}

\subsection{How the reversal is taught}\label{sec:tier}
The eight conventions we instruct differ sharply in how their \emph{reversal} is
represented in the material models learn from, and this, more than anything else,
predicts what models do. We code each convention on a three-level ordinal scale by
auditing standard textbooks (the audited list and coding criteria are in
Appendix~\ref{app:audit}). \emph{Tier 0}: the reversal is taught as a routine
operation. Reversing a contour's orientation negates the integral; this is a theorem
statement in every calculus text, with exercises. Reaction forces and inward normals
are likewise curriculum material. \emph{Tier 1}: the reversal appears only as an
error contrast or a partial treatment; every introductory physics text warns that
using the left hand gives the opposite direction, which teaches the flip while
forbidding it. \emph{Tier 2}: the reversal is essentially absent. We could not find
a single worked counter-clockwise mesh example in any circuits text we audited, and
our own search for a second tier-2 convention failed (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}).

Table~\ref{tab:entrench} and Figure~\ref{fig:entrench} show frontier reversion at
low difficulty against this scale. The separation is stark. Tier-0 conventions are
obeyed at \cwRate--\rxnRate\%. The single tier-2 convention is disobeyed at
\ccwRate\%. Tier-1 conventions fall between or split by model. A weighted logistic
regression of reversion on tier across all eight conventions yields $z=\tierZ$. We
emphasize what this gradient means: the models are not incapable of sign-flipped
reporting, since they perform the identical transform when the convention naming it
is one whose reversal they have seen taught. What they lack is any trained
representation of \emph{this} reversal, and no instruction we found supplies it at
inference time (Section~\ref{sec:nofix}).

\subsection{How hard the problem is}\label{sec:dose}
Obedience on taught-reversal conventions is not unconditional; it has a load limit.
Within the contour domain, we harden the problems (higher polynomial degree, more
polygon edges) and measure reversion as each model's competence strain rises, where
strain is simply the fraction of default-cell problems whose magnitudes the model
now gets wrong. Reversion on the orientation convention climbs monotonically:
gpt-oss from \cwEGptOss\% to \cwHGptOss\% at \strainHGptOss\% strain, GPT-5 from
\cwEGptFive\% to \cwHGptFive\% at \strainHGptFive\%, and Gemma from \cwEGemma\% to
\cwHGemma\% at \strainHGemma\% (a five-fold resampling of this last, largest figure
gives mean 34.4\%, SD 6.4 points). The crucial comparison is the \texttt{wrk}
control cell, which demands the identical negation of the same variables: it stays
below 1\% for every frontier model at every difficulty
(Figure~\ref{fig:dose}; weighted logistic $z=\strainZ$). Difficulty does not cause
generic instruction-forgetting. It selectively reactivates the trained default of
the specific convention under pressure. One consequence is worth stating plainly:
the failure is worst exactly where users need the model most, on hard problems.
(Rows lost to truncation in the hardened tier are the most-strained instances, so
observed rates are, if anything, underestimates.)

\subsection{How capable the model is}\label{sec:cap}
The pedagogy gradient of Section~\ref{sec:tier} describes frontier models. Below
the frontier, compliance degrades even on tier-0 conventions: the Qwen3 family
reverts on contour orientation at 91--99\% at every scale and in both reasoning
modes (Table~\ref{tab:c1}), and DeepSeek-V3, Llama-3.3-70B, and Qwen2.5-72B revert
at 92--94\%. Even at this floor, convention identity still matters: the same weak
models revert far less on the transform-matched \texttt{wrk} cell (for example, 92\%
versus 35\% for DeepSeek-V3), so the gradient's fingerprint is visible below the
capability threshold even though absolute compliance is not achievable there.
Following a taught reversal under load is, itself, an emergent capability.

\section{What mitigation does and does not do}\label{sec:nofix}

\subsection{Reasoning modes: large competence gains, conditional compliance}
The Qwen3 family can run with its reasoning mode on or off at identical weights,
which isolates the effect of test-time deliberation. On contour, the toggle raises
magnitude-competence from 0--15\% to 24--88\% across five scales while moving
orientation reversion by two to four points (Table~\ref{tab:c1}); the same
dissociation holds in circuits. Deliberation, by itself, does not make the
instruction win.

The vectors domain, however, reveals the boundary of this negative result, and we
report it because it sharpens rather than weakens the claim. On the handedness
convention, reasoning \emph{does} restore compliance: reversion falls from
\LhNothinkMid\% to \LhThinkMid\% at 4B and to \LhThinkBig\% at 8B
(Table~\ref{tab:rhrladder}, Appendix~\ref{app:rhrladder}). In the same responses,
the reaction-reporting convention keeps slipping at \RxnThinkBig--\LhThinkSmall\%.
The pattern across all three domains is consistent: test-time deliberation can
\emph{retrieve} a reversal that is taught and procedurally shallow, a per-operation
sign rule the chain of thought can state and apply each time it is needed. It does
not conjure a reversal that was never taught (circuits), it does not sustain one
that must be maintained across every step of a long derivation (contour
orientation under load), and it does not reliably hold a framing convention that
applies once at reporting time (reaction). What compute buys is retrieval, not
override.

\subsection{Worked demonstrations: almost nothing}
If reversion reflects a missing training representation, perhaps supplying that
representation in-context cures it. We prepend one or three fully worked,
solver-verified examples of counter-clockwise mesh analysis (held-out problems,
different topologies) to the same circuit instances. The result is a nearly flat
dose-response (Figure~\ref{fig:kshot}): Gemma declines modestly
(\shotZeroGemma\%$\to$\shotOneGemma\%$\to$\shotThreeGemma\%, about 4.5 points per
example), while gpt-oss (\shotZeroGptOss\%$\to$\shotThreeGptOss\%) and GPT-5
(\shotZeroGptFive\%$\to$\shotOneGptFive\%$\to$\shotThreeGptFive\%) do not move at
all. Reading a correct demonstration of exactly the required behavior, immediately
before the problem, does not override the prior. The mitigation practitioners would
reach for first is the one we can most confidently say does not work.

\section{Two pre-registered predictions, both refuted}\label{sec:prereg}
Before collecting the corresponding data, we committed two predictions to the
project's version history. \textbf{Prediction 1}: left-handed cross products would
behave like counter-clockwise mesh currents, an untrained reversal, and frontier
models would revert heavily. \emph{Refuted}: across three difficulty tiers and
roughly 3{,}700 diagnostic variables, frontier reversion on the left-hand rule is
\lhGptOss--\lhGptFive\% ($0/\lhNGemma$ for Gemma at 95\% competence). In hindsight
the pedagogy explains the miss: every textbook teaches the left-hand outcome as an
explicit warning, which is training signal for the flip. This refutation is what
forced the ordinal coding of Section~\ref{sec:tier}, and our subsequent failure to
find any second tier-2 convention is itself evidence for how unusual the mesh
convention's pedagogical silence is. \textbf{Prediction 2}: in-context
demonstrations would collapse reversion if it stems from a missing representation.
\emph{Refuted}, as Section~\ref{sec:nofix} shows. We report both because a
measurement framework that only ever confirms its authors' expectations is not
measuring anything; the claims that survive are the ones these refutations
disciplined.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
Instruction-following benchmarks grade compliance with formatting and content
constraints \citep{zhou2023ifeval}; counter-default instructions have been studied
as inverse instructions \citep{stephan2024inverse} and counterfactual task variants
\citep{wu2024reasoning}. Relative to these, our contribution is machinery that
separates compliance from competence \emph{within} a single response using
sign-exact oracles, plus controls (transform-matched cells, procedure contracts)
that localize the failure to convention identity. Sycophancy work documents
deference to user framing \citep{sharma2023sycophancy}; reversion persists with no
user stance to defer to. Test-time-scaling studies measure capability gains from
deliberation \citep{muennighoff2025s1,deepseekr1}; we show those gains transfer to
compliance only under narrow conditions. The benchmark extends a published
single-domain probe \citep{ravishankara2026circuchain} and follows deterministic
evaluation practice from engineering-plot benchmarks
\citep{ravishankara2026plotchain}; canary-based contamination protection follows
\citet{srivastava2022bigbench}; statistics use exact McNemar tests
\citep{mcnemar1947} and Wilson intervals \citep{wilson1927}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Implications for evaluation.} Any benchmark that grades magnitudes, accepts
answers modulo sign, or scores with a language-model judge will award full credit to
every reverted answer in this study. Sign-exact grading against the instructed
frame is cheap when the domain permits it, and our results suggest it measures a
dimension of controllability that accuracy does not.

\textbf{Implications for deployment.} The failure mode is silent, directed toward
the training distribution, load-sensitive, and robust to prompting. In engineering
use, a deterministic frame-check layer (recompute the requested convention's signs
from the model's own magnitudes) costs almost nothing and, on this evidence, is
worth more than any reasoning budget.

\textbf{Limitations.} The tier-2 pole of the pedagogy scale is occupied by a single
convention; we searched for a second and found only a tier-1 case, so we present a
measured gradient rather than a two-point law. Difficulty could not be raised
enough in the vectors domain to strain frontier models, so the taught-reversal
$\times$ high-load cell is measured only in contour. Pedagogy tiers are coded from
a finite audit of standard textbooks (Appendix~\ref{app:audit}). Our analysis is
behavioral; we do not localize the mechanism inside the network. Serving stacks are
mixed across the panel, though pinned and recorded, and hardened-tier truncation
censors the most-strained rows in the conservative direction.

\textbf{Release.} The generators, oracles, validators, grading, and analysis code
are released publicly; every dataset regenerates byte-identically from a seed, so
the public artifact is a one-command generator rather than a crawlable answer key.
Frozen instances and model outputs are available under gated access with embedded
canaries. We take this structure seriously because this benchmark measures a
training-frequency effect: if its instances and instructed answers entered a future
training corpus, they would not merely inflate scores, they would administer the
treatment and invert the measurement.

\section{Textbook audit behind the pedagogy tiers}\label{app:audit}
For each convention we examined the treatment of its \emph{reversal} in standard
textbooks: for circuits, Nilsson \& Riedel; Hayt, Kemmerly \& Durbin; Irwin \&
Nelms; Sadiku; and Kuphaldt's open text; for multivariable calculus, Stewart;
Thomas; Marsden \& Tromba; and the OpenStax Calculus series; for mechanics and
electromagnetism, Halliday, Resnick \& Walker; Serway \& Jewett; Young \& Freedman;
Griffiths; and OpenStax University Physics. A convention's reversal is coded
\emph{tier 0} if at least one audited text states it as a rule or theorem and at
least one provides exercises using it (orientation reversal, reaction pairs, inward
normals, work against a field); \emph{tier 1} if audited texts mention the reversal
only as an error contrast or partial treatment without worked examples (left-hand
rule warnings, active sign convention, non-ground references); \emph{tier 2} if no
audited text provides any worked treatment of the reversal (counter-clockwise mesh
currents; we found none). The coding predates the vectors-domain results
(Section~\ref{sec:prereg} describes how a mis-anticipation of the left-hand rule's
tier motivated this audit).

\section{Reproducibility}\label{app:repro}
All datasets are deterministic functions of a configuration file and a seed;
regeneration commands, manifests with canary identifiers, transform-validation
reports (125/125 per domain), the complete statistical freeze backing every number
in this paper, and per-response provenance (model identifier, serving stack,
quantization, sampling parameters) accompany the code. No language model is
involved at any point in generation, verification, grading, or analysis.
